# Wizard multiplayer on bad internet: what I measured overnight, and what I built

Written for Jorge, morning of Sunday 2026-09-20. Everything here was measured on real Chrome sessions playing real games, three players per game, six rounds per run, with every connection pushed through a link conditioner that caps bandwidth and adds delay. Numbers are from the runs themselves; where I could not measure something I say so.

## The short version

- The current game is slow on a bad link mostly because of round trips, not because of the size of what it downloads. Firestore compresses its channel, so a play costs a few KB even late in a game, not the 50 KB I estimated from the document size on Friday. That estimate was wrong and this note corrects it.
- The slimmed Firestore version (finished rounds archived off the hot document) cuts the bytes per play in half and cuts the worst-case downloads by about four times. It changes little about latency on a merely slow link, and helps more on a starved one. It is a strict improvement, it is tested, and it ships either way.
- A server-authoritative game on a Cloudflare Durable Object is the genuinely better transport: a play reaches the table in 40 to 60 percent of the time Firestore needs on the same link (one round trip instead of two), 15 to 20 times fewer bytes of payload and far fewer on the wire, and a reconnect the app controls. The prototype is real and measured on the real edge, but it is a prototype: no lobby, identity, bots, undo, votes, chat, presence or history. Bringing it to feature parity is a multi-day port, not an overnight one.

## What the numbers mean

**Latency is round trips, and Firestore needs two.** On the slow3g link (400 ms round trip) a play reaches the other players in about 750 ms on the current game and about 470 ms on the prototype. On the awful link (800 ms round trip) it is about 2.1 s versus 0.8 s. That ratio is not about bytes. A Firestore play is an HTTP write that has to complete a full round trip before the server pushes the change down the listen channel; a WebSocket play is one message up and one down. The slim version cannot change that, and its latency gains are small (about 5 to 10 percent), exactly as expected.

**Bytes were not the villain I said they were.** On Friday I told you each play re-downloads a document that is 25 to 60 KB and estimated 2 MB per round per phone. The document size was right; the conclusion was wrong. Firestore compresses its channel, so the measured cost of a play on the current game is 3 to 5 KB by round 6, with occasional spikes of 100 KB or more when the channel resynchronises. The slim build halves the typical cost and caps the spikes at about 10 KB, because the document it resends is only the current round. The prototype sends 140 to 250 bytes of payload per play and, on the wire with WebSocket compression, tens of bytes.

**Where the slim build earns its keep.** Two places. First, the spikes: a 125 KB resync on a 12 KB/s link is ten seconds of nothing; on the slim build that worst case is under one second. Second, late rounds and bigger tables: my runs stopped at round 6 of a 3-player game. A 5-player game at round 12 has a current-build document five to six times the size, and every byte of it is resent per play, while the slim document stays about the same size all game.

**Reconnecting is where Firestore is weakest.** After an 8 second dropout the cut-off phone took between 3.5 and 23 seconds to be able to act again on the current and slim builds. That time is Firestore's own reconnect back-off and I cannot tune it from the app. The prototype came back in about 5 seconds, and that number is mine to change: the client pings every 3 seconds, declares the link dead at 6, and retries with a back-off that I capped at 5 seconds. A tighter cap makes it 2 seconds.

## What I built and where it is

- The slim Firestore version is in the app source: finished rounds are archived to `rooms/{code}/rounds/{n}` in the same write that deals the next round, the room document keeps only the current round plus the light per-round entries the live screens read, and everything that needs the whole game (History, the recap, the round-by-round table) stitches the archives back together. Rules for the new subcollection are deployed. Old rooms and games that straddle the change still read whole.
- A Durable Object prototype in `netlab-do/`, deployed to your personal Cloudflare account as `wizard-netlab-do` for measurement only. Server-authoritative rules reusing the app's own engine, WebSocket hibernation, small events, full state on reconnect, ping-based dead-link detection. It plays a complete game of Wizard and nothing else: no lobby, identity, bots, undo, votes, chat, presence or history.
- The test rig in `scripts/netlab/`: three real headless Chrome sessions play real games against the production bundle while a userland link conditioner shapes every connection. It is reusable for any future change; the repo notes explain how, and the one rule that cost me an hour tonight: run one rig at a time.

## Two corrections to things I said earlier

1. The 2 MB per round figure was an overestimate by roughly ten times. The document is that big; the wire is not.
2. Chrome's built-in network throttling does not limit the bandwidth of long-lived streams, which is why the first hour of measurements tonight had to be thrown away and redone through a TCP-level shaper. Any future measurement should use the shaper.

## Recommendation

Ship the slim build now. It is done, measured, and strictly better on every axis I tested, and it is the data model the Durable Object version would want anyway.

Then decide about the port with the family's real experience in mind. If the complaint was mostly "every play takes forever", the Durable Object roughly halves that and is the real fix, and I would start it: it is a few days of work to reach parity with what the game does today, and the risky part, the transport, is now proven on the real edge. If the complaint was mostly "the game froze when someone's Wi-Fi dropped", the honest answer is that both stacks pause while a phone is offline, and the difference is a controllable 2 to 5 second reconnect versus Firestore's 3 to 23 seconds.

I did not start the full port overnight. Doing it half-way would have left you with two games and neither finished.
